Cross-cutting · the machines

Robot Types — the actual machines

The other five tabs are the technology layers inside a robot. This tab is the complementary axis: the physical robot forms you actually buy — classified by mechanical structure (the six IFR industrial types) and by mobility. A Cartesian/XYZ gantry, a SCARA, a delta, a humanoid… each combines the five layers differently. Photos load from Wikimedia Commons; a diagram shows if a photo can't load.

Industrial robots — by mechanical structure (the IFR six)
6-axis · articulated
Articulated arm
The classic robot arm with rotary joints — the most common industrial robot. Largest reach and flexibility per footprint.
Best for Welding, assembly, painting, machine tending — automotive & general mfg.
4-axis · planar
SCARA
Rigid vertically, compliant horizontally. Very fast and repeatable for flat pick-and-place and vertical insertion.
Best for Electronics assembly, small-parts pick-and-place, packaging.
3-axis · linear (XYZ)
Cartesian / gantry
Linear motion on three perpendicular axes (X-Y-Z); large ones are "gantry" robots. Very high precision and stiffness over a rectangular volume.
Best for Large-part handling, palletizing, CNC / 3D-printing, deep-reach tasks.
3–4 axis · parallel
Delta / parallel
Overhead "spider" with arms on a shared platform. The fastest cycle times of any type, for light objects.
Best for High-speed sorting & packaging — food, pharma, consumer goods.
rotary + linear
Cylindrical
An arm working within a cylindrical envelope (rotary base + vertical + reach). Compact but limited; a declining legacy niche.
Best for Machine tending, simple vertical assembly in tight spaces.
functional class
Collaborative (cobot)
A safety class, not a shape (usually articulated): force-limited to work beside humans without cages. Fast to deploy, lower payload/speed.
Best for High-mix / low-volume SMEs, machine tending, labs.
Mobile & service robots — by locomotion & form
wheeled · mobile
AGV / AMR
Wheeled transport robots. AGVs follow fixed guides; AMRs navigate freely with onboard sensing + SLAM. Efficient on flat floors, can't climb.
Best for Warehousing, e-commerce fulfillment, intralogistics, hospitals.
bipedal · human form
Humanoid
Two legs, human shape — built to work in environments and with tools made for humans. Max versatility, but the hardest balance/cost problem.
Best for Human-built spaces; logistics/mfg pilots today (emerging).
four-legged
Quadruped
Robot "dogs" — stable on rough, uneven terrain and stairs where wheels fail. Expensive, energy-hungry, limited payload.
Best for Industrial inspection, security patrol, hazardous-site mapping.
aerial · UAV
Drone (UAV)
Aerial robots — access from above, cover large areas fast, cheap per area. Limited flight time/payload; weather- and airspace-constrained.
Best for Inspection, agriculture, surveying, delivery, defense.
medical · teleoperated
Surgical robot
Teleoperated precision manipulators (e.g. da Vinci) for minimally-invasive surgery. Sub-mm precision; very high cost; long regulatory cycles.
Best for Precision-critical, minimally-invasive healthcare procedures.

Perception — how robots see & sense

Sensing the environment and interpreting it into a usable model of the world. The central truth: no single sensor is enough — modern robots run sensor fusion, and AI is increasingly the layer that makes cheap sensors "see" as well as expensive ones.

The sensing technologieshow each works · pros · cons · best fit
📷RGB camera (2D vision)
A normal image sensor + AI (neural nets) to recognize what's in the frame.
+Cheap, information-rich (color, text, texture); most mature AI stack.
–No native distance; struggles in low light and glare.
Best for  Object recognition, inspection, reading labels.
👓Stereo camera
Two lenses a known distance apart; depth from the disparity between images — like human eyes.
+Depth + color, fully passive (emits nothing).
–Fails on textureless surfaces and in darkness.
Best for  Legged-robot navigation, general depth (e.g. Spot).
🌀Depth camera (RGB-D)
RGB plus an active emitter — structured light or time-of-flight — measuring distance per pixel.
+Accurate short-range depth and recognition together.
–Short range (metres); degrades in direct sunlight.
Best for  Grasping, pick-and-place, indoor robots.
📡LiDAR
Fires laser pulses and times the reflection → a precise 3D point cloud of the scene.
+Very accurate 3D maps; works in darkness; long range.
–Costly; degrades in fog/rain; no color.
Best for  Autonomous navigation, mapping (SLAM), AVs.
〰️Radar
Radio waves instead of light; measures range and velocity directly.
+Works through fog/rain/dust; measures speed.
–Low resolution — can't resolve fine shapes.
Best for  Outdoor, adverse weather, high-speed detection.
✋Tactile / force sensing
"Skin" on fingers and grippers measuring pressure and force.
+Enables grasping without crushing; feel texture/slip.
–Immature, expensive; sits between sensing & manipulation.
Best for  Fine manipulation, fragile objects.
Metrics that matter
Range — cm to 200m+ Resolution — pixels / points-per-second Accuracy — depth error in mm Field of view — degrees Frame rate — Hz / latency Cost / unit — $ at volume Power draw — W Robustness — light / weather

⚑ The debate that decides winners

Rich sensor suite (LiDAR-led) vs. vision-only (cameras + AI). As AI gets better at inferring depth from plain images, the case for expensive LiDAR weakens — yet unit demand for sensors from humanoids and AVs keeps rising even as the LiDAR industry consolidates.

Vision-only camp

Cameras are cheap and scale; the intelligence moves to software. Bet: AI closes the gap (Tesla's approach).

vs
Sensor-fusion camp

Redundancy = safety; LiDAR gives ground-truth 3D that AI can't yet guarantee. Bet: hardware reliability wins (Waymo).

Whoever's right reshapes a multi-billion-dollar sensor market and decides whether LiDAR is a growth industry or a squeezed one.

◆ Investment implications
  • Image-sensor oligopoly — Sony/OmniVision sell to every camera-based robot regardless of who wins; a classic toll on the vision layer.
  • LiDAR is consolidating — treat pure-play LiDAR names as high-variance; the vision-only thesis is the tail risk to underwrite.
  • Watch the camera-only inflection — each step-change in AI depth estimation is bearish for premium sensors and bullish for compute/software.
  • Tactile sensing is an emerging category — early, but a prerequisite for humanoid dexterity (bridges into Layer 4).

Layer 2 · The muscles

Actuation — how robots move

Converting energy into precise, controllable motion. This is the most concentrated and defensible layer in the whole stack — a handful of suppliers own the critical components, and demand from humanoids is running into real capacity limits.

The actuation technologies
🔩Precision reducers / gearboxes
Gearing that trades motor speed for joint torque. Harmonic & RV (cycloidal) are the high-precision types; the single most critical component.
+Enable precise, high-torque, near-zero-backlash joints.
–Capacity-constrained; long lead times; a genuine bottleneck.
Best for  Every articulated joint — the choke point of the whole industry.
🧲Electric motors (BLDC / servo)
Brushless motors with encoders and controllers; the default "muscle" of modern robots.
+Precise, clean, software-controllable, reliable.
–Lower peak force-per-size than hydraulics.
Best for  Almost all robots today, including humanoids.
📦Integrated actuators
Motor + reducer + controller + sensor packaged as one "joint module."
+Faster development; compact; popular with humanoid startups.
–Less optimization freedom; still depends on reducer supply.
Best for  Rapid humanoid prototyping and volume scaling.
💧Hydraulic
Pressurized fluid drives pistons — brute force and explosive motion.
+Highest force density; fast, powerful movements.
–Heavy, leaky, hard to control, costly.
Best for  Heavy machinery, legacy high-power robots (old Atlas).
💨Pneumatic
Compressed air actuators — light and compliant.
+Cheap, lightweight, inherently "soft"/safe.
–Imprecise; needs a compressor.
Best for  Simple grippers, soft robotics.
🧬Soft / novel actuators
Shape-memory alloys, electroactive polymers, artificial-muscle concepts.
+Light, human-safe, compliant.
–Experimental; limited force/durability today.
Best for  Next-gen soft/wearable robotics (watch-list).
Metrics that matter
Torque density — Nm per kg Backlash — arc-min (lower = tighter) Efficiency — % energy to motion Repeatability — ±mm Backdrivability — force sensitivity Peak vs. continuous — torque Cost / joint — $
⚑ The debate that decides winners

Does the Japanese reducer duopoly hold, or does China/vertical integration break it? Harmonic Drive (~85%) and Nabtesco (~60%) own the precision-reducer choke point, and their capacity is a live constraint as humanoids scale — a textbook pricing-power position.

Moat holds

Precision reducers are decades of metallurgy & process know-how; hard to replicate. Incumbents capture the humanoid boom.

vs
Moat erodes

China (Leader Drive, Sanhua) localizes cheaper reducers; humanoid makers integrate actuators in-house to escape the bottleneck.

The answer sets margins across the entire humanoid supply chain — this is the clearest single lever in the stack.

◆ Investment implications
  • The clearest "picks & shovels" — reducers and precision motors sell to every robot maker, humanoid or industrial; a supply constraint is pricing power.
  • Watch China localization — the key swing factor; erosion of the Japanese reducer moat is the main downside to the incumbent thesis.
  • Integrated-actuator makers — a faster-growing, more competitive niche; benefit from humanoid volume but with thinner differentiation.
  • Low AI-disruption risk — this is hardware/metallurgy; value accrues to whoever can physically build precision at volume.

The intelligence sub-layers
🔎Perception AI
Computer-vision models that turn raw sensor data into recognized objects, poses and scenes.
+Very mature; can substitute for expensive sensors.
–Edge cases & safety guarantees still hard.
Best for  Making cheap cameras "see" — links back to Layer 1.
🎮Control & learning (RL / imitation)
Reinforcement & imitation learning teach balance, walking and manipulation instead of hand-coding them.
+Handles dynamic tasks (walking, recovery) impossible to script.
–Data-hungry; sim-to-real gap; hard to certify.
Best for  Legged locomotion, dexterous manipulation.
🌐Foundation models (VLA)
Large vision-language-action models aiming to be a "GPT for robots" — one model, many tasks.
+Generalization; a single brain across tasks/robots.
–Early; reliability & data scarcity unresolved.
Best for  The long-term general-purpose robot bet.
🕹️Simulation & sim-to-real
Virtual worlds where robots train millions of times faster than reality, then transfer to hardware.
+Cheap, safe, massively parallel training.
–The "reality gap" — sim ≠ real physics.
Best for  Training locomotion/manipulation at scale (NVIDIA Isaac).
💽Edge compute
On-board chips running inference in real time within the robot's power budget.
+Low latency; no cloud dependency.
–Compute vs. power/heat trade-off (ties to Layer 5).
Best for  Real-time autonomy on mobile robots.
🧭Navigation & SLAM
Simultaneous localization and mapping — build a map while tracking position in it.
+Mature; enables autonomous mobile robots (AMRs).
–Dynamic, crowded environments still challenge it.
Best for  Warehouse AMRs, autonomous navigation.
Metrics that matter
Generalization — tasks per model Data efficiency — demos to learn a task Sim-to-real gap — transfer success % Inference latency — ms Success rate — % task completion Compute per action — TOPS / joules
⚑ The debate that decides winners

Who owns the "robot brain" layer — vertical hardware makers or horizontal model providers? This is the highest-uncertainty, highest-upside question in the entire stack, and the one most analogous to the LLM platform wars.

Vertical / integrated

Tesla, Figure own hardware and brain — data flywheel from their own fleet; tight hardware-software co-design.

vs
Horizontal / model layer

Physical Intelligence, Google DeepMind sell a foundation model to all robot makers — the "Android for robots" play.

And underneath both: NVIDIA collects a toll on training/simulation regardless of who wins the application layer.

Key playersblue = structural toll position
NVIDIACompute + Isaac/Omniverse — universal
Google DeepMindGemini Robotics (VLA models)
Physical IntelligenceRobot foundation models

◆ Investment implications
  • NVIDIA is the toll booth — nearly everyone trains and simulates on its stack; the lowest-variance way to own the AI layer.
  • The foundation-model layer is the wild card — highest upside, highest uncertainty; watch whether a horizontal "robot GPT" or vertical stacks win.
  • Data is the real moat — real-world robot interaction data is scarce; fleets in the field (Tesla, Figure, Amazon) compound an advantage.
  • This layer re-prices the others — better AI commoditizes sensors (Layer 1) and raises the value of data & compute.

The end-effector technologies
🤏2-finger / parallel grippers
Two jaws that open and close — the workhorse of industrial automation.
+Cheap, reliable, simple to control.
–Limited to objects that fit the jaw; no dexterity.
Best for  Pick-and-place of known, uniform parts.
🌬️Suction / vacuum
A vacuum cup grips flat or smooth surfaces.
+Fast, gentle, great for boxes/sheets/glass.
–Needs a flat, non-porous, sealable surface.
Best for  Logistics, packaging, warehouse picking.
🫧Soft grippers
Compliant, often air-driven fingers that conform to the object.
+Handle irregular, delicate, variable items safely.
–Lower precision & force; slower.
Best for  Food handling, fragile/mixed goods.
🖐️Multi-finger dexterous hands
Human-like hands with many actuated joints — the humanoid end-goal.
+Can (in principle) use human tools and do any manual task.
–Very expensive, fragile, hard to control — the bottleneck.
Best for  General-purpose humanoids (still maturing).
👆Tactile skin / sensing
Pressure/force sensors on fingertips giving a sense of touch and slip.
+Enables grasping fragile items without crushing.
–Immature; durability and cost unproven.
Best for  Fine, feedback-driven manipulation (bridges Layer 1).
🔧Task-specific tools
Welders, screwdrivers, dispensers bolted to the arm for one job.
+Optimal performance and cost for a fixed task.
–Zero flexibility — one tool, one job.
Best for  High-volume industrial lines.
Metrics that matter
Degrees of freedom — actuated joints Payload — kg grasped Grip force — N, and control range Cycle time — grasps/min Tactile resolution — sensing points Object generality — variety handled Cost & durability — $ / cycles
⚑ The debate that decides winners

Is a general-purpose 5-finger hand necessary — or is it over-engineering? Dexterous hands are where humanoid cost, fragility and unreliability concentrate. The answer shapes the humanoid bill-of-materials and timeline.

Dexterity is essential

To do human jobs in human spaces with human tools, robots need human-like hands + touch. Solve it and the market unlocks.

vs
Simpler wins first

Most valuable tasks need only 2–3 fingers or swappable tools. Chasing full dexterity delays useful, cheaper robots.

Whichever proves true determines whether humanoids are near-term products or a longer-dated dream.

◆ Investment implications
  • The gating factor for humanoid utility — progress (or lack of it) on dexterous hands is a leading indicator for the whole humanoid thesis.
  • Industrial grippers are a stable niche — mature, profitable, but not where the exponential upside is.
  • Tactile sensing is an emerging pick-and-shovel — a prerequisite for dexterity; a small category that could inflect.
  • Much value is being captured in-house — leading humanoid makers build their own hands, limiting the merchant-supplier opportunity.

Layer 5 · The foundation

Power — the endurance constraint

The most overlooked layer, and often the binding one. Energy storage and delivery cap runtime, payload, force and how autonomous a mobile robot can really be — a humanoid that runs a couple of hours per charge isn't yet an all-day worker.

The power technologies
🔋Li-ion battery packs
The default energy store, largely borrowed from the EV and electronics supply chain.
+Mature, scalable, falling $/kWh from EV volumes.
–Energy density caps runtime; adds weight & heat.
Best for  Virtually all mobile robots today.
🧮Battery management (BMS)
Electronics that balance cells, manage charge/heat and protect the pack.
+Extends life & safety; enables fast/safe charging.
–Adds complexity; a safety-critical component.
Best for  Any serious battery-powered robot.
⚡Power electronics
Converters/inverters that feed motors and compute at the right voltage — increasingly SiC/GaN.
+Higher efficiency = more runtime from the same pack.
–Thermal management under peak actuator loads.
Best for  High-power actuation and on-board compute.
🔌Tethered / hot-swap / docking
Cable power, swappable packs, or auto-charging docks to sidestep runtime limits.
+Effectively unlimited uptime in fixed settings.
–Constrains mobility or needs infrastructure/logistics.
Best for  Warehouses, fixed-route operations.
🧊Solid-state & next-gen cells
New chemistries promising higher energy density and safety.
+Could materially extend runtime and cut weight.
–Not yet at scale/cost; timeline uncertain.
Best for  A watch-list unlock for humanoid endurance.
🌡️Thermal management
Cooling for actuators, batteries and compute working near their limits.
+Sustains peak performance; protects components.
–Adds weight/space; a design constraint everywhere.
Best for  Dense humanoids under continuous load.
Metrics that matter
Energy density — Wh/kg Power density — W/kg (peak draw) Runtime — hours per charge Charge time — to 80% Cycle life — charges before decay Efficiency — % delivered to work Thermal envelope — °C sustained
⚑ The debate that decides winners

Is power a solved problem or the real ceiling on humanoids? Batteries improve steadily — not exponentially like AI — so energy can quietly gate adoption even when the "brain" is ready.

Not a blocker

EV-driven cost/density gains + hot-swap and docking make runtime a manageable operations problem, not a wall.

vs
The binding constraint

All-day humanoid work needs a step-change in Wh/kg; until then, robots are tethered, part-time, or logistics-heavy.

This layer sets the realistic duty cycle — and therefore the labor economics — of every mobile robot.

Key playerslargely the EV battery complex
CATL / BYDBattery cells at scale
LG / Samsung SDI / PanasonicCells & packs
Power-semi (Infineon, onsemi)SiC/GaN power electronics
Solid-state startupsNext-gen density (watch-list)
◆ Investment implications
  • Mostly a shared EV supply chain — robotics is an incremental demand source on top of autos, not a standalone battery thesis (yet).
  • Power density is the silent gate — track Wh/kg and runtime as a reality-check on aggressive humanoid-deployment forecasts.
  • Power electronics is a quieter pick-and-shovel — efficiency gains (SiC/GaN) directly extend runtime; benefits from both EV and robot demand.
  • Solid-state is the optional unlock — a genuine step-change here would re-rate humanoid timelines; keep on the watch-list.

Phase 2 · Industry structure — a living evidence board

Value Chain — who captures the value

The layer-tabs answer how a robot works; this tab asks who captures the value. Rather than assert a made-up ranking, it is built to accumulate evidence over time — every claim carries its source, and what isn't proven yet is flagged as a hypothesis, not a number. Today it rests on three anchors: IFR's player taxonomy, sourced concentration data, and Bloomberg SPLC supply-chain links.

World Robotics 2025 defines the industrial chain as component suppliers → robot producers → distributors → system integrators → end users — so the staging isn't ad-hoc. What IFR does not do is split the margin; it counts installations (demand). Everything below is the attempt to ground that missing economics, piece by piece.

⚑ Working hypothesis — not yet fully evidenced

Value pools at the two IP-rich ends (components, compute) and thins through assembly and integration. So far the evidence supports the components end (SPLC: the reducer maker invoices every OEM). The compute end and the integration trough still need grounding in real margins — that's the next data pull, not a number to assert today.

Bottlenecks win

Supply-constrained components + compute sell to everyone; the humanoid brand is a crowded, capital-hungry race. Partly evidenced.

vs
The platform wins

As with phones / cars, the integrator that nails AI + brand captures the economics and commoditizes suppliers. Unproven until a humanoid ships.

◆ What the evidence says today
  • The reducer bottleneck is proven, not asserted — Bloomberg SPLC shows Nabtesco invoicing FANUC, ABB, Kawasaki, KUKA and Yaskawa. The cleanest pricing-power node — but a Japanese-duopoly exposure to monitor vs. Chinese entrants.
  • NVIDIA is the brand-agnostic toll — concentration is clear; the dollar linkage into robot OEMs is the next thing to evidence.
  • Watch the purity trap — Moog, Timken and Tesla screen as robotics but their disclosed business is aerospace, industrial and autos. Pure-plays read cleaner.
  • Still open: pool sizing and the humanoid economics — SPLC can't see Optimus-class BOMs yet. To be grounded when the data arrives.

◆ How to use this list
  • The public "picks & shovels" cluster is the cleanest entry — reducers (Japan), compute (NVIDIA), sensors — where concentration meets a listing.
  • The humanoid frontier is a private-market map — Tesla and UBTECH are the main listed pure-ish plays; the rest is venture. Watch for IPOs (Unitree, Figure).
  • Geography is a real axis — Japan/Europe own components & arms, the US owns compute/AI, China is scaling both low-cost components and humanoids.
